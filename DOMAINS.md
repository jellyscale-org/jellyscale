# Domains

The terms, boundaries and invariants of the system. Reviewers check changes against this file. If a change needs one of these to bend, update this file in the same change and say why.

## Vocabulary

- **Role:** what a process does, chosen by `--roles`: `api`, `stream` or `worker`. One binary, many Deployments.
- **Library:** a configured collection with a media store and an asset store. Clients see it as a `CollectionFolder`.
- **Item:** a Movie, Series, Season or Episode. Identified by a stable UUID.
- **Media source:** the playable object behind an item.
- **Store:** a POSIX or S3-compatible backend, reached only through `internal/store`.
- **Session:** a device's token, plus what it's playing.
- **Alias:** an obsolete route kept only for jellyfin-web.
- **Hint:** a NOTIFY wakeup. Lossy by design.
- **Spec hash:** identifies one transcode output: item, source and its ETag, audio track, video and audio bitrate and resolution as ffmpeg will use them, segment length, encoder family and the output settings version. A burnt-in subtitle track joins it, and so does whether the video is copied.
- **Segment:** a 4 second piece of a transcode, stored as `segments/{spec_hash}/{n}.mp4`, with `init.mp4` beside them. In a copied stream it runs from a keyframe to the next one that makes it 4 seconds long, or to the last one that keeps it within 12 seconds, so it can be shorter than 4 seconds (invariant 47).
- **Copied stream:** a transcode that sends the source's video as it is and re-encodes only the audio, because the client plays the video but not the audio or the container.
- **Keyframe index:** the times of a source's keyframes, read from the file's own index at scan time and stored in `media_sources.keyframes`. A copied stream is cut on it.
- **Transcode store:** the store segments live in, configured once for every role that makes or serves segments.
- **Job:** one ffmpeg run that makes segments from N on. Workers claim jobs from a Postgres table.
- **Viewer:** one play session watching a spec. Jobs live while viewers do.
- **Seam:** where segments from two jobs meet.

## Boundaries

| Package | Owns | Must not |
|---|---|---|
| `internal/api` | HTTP, auth, DTO shaping at the edge | Touch a store except through `internal/store` |
| `internal/items` | Item queries and hydration | Know about HTTP |
| `internal/library` | Scanning, naming, probing, the keyframe index | Serve requests |
| `internal/store` | Backend differences | Leak backend types or credentials |
| `internal/playback` | The decision between direct play and transcode, the spec and its hash, and the playlists | Do I/O |
| `internal/transcode` | Jobs, viewers, ffmpeg and the segment store | Know about HTTP |
| `internal/subtitles` | Extracting a text track from a file, its formats and time shift, and its cache in the asset store (the jobs that fill it are in `internal/transcode`) | Know about HTTP |
| `internal/playstate` | Watch state: position, played, favorite, and its write rules | Know about HTTP or sessions |
| `internal/api` session persistence (`sessions`, `session_sockets`) | What each device is playing and which sockets it holds open | Keep that state in process memory |
| `internal/compat` | Client conventions | Hold domain logic |
| `internal/db` | Pool and migrations | Hold queries for other domains |

## Invariants

### Scaling

1. No process keeps state another replica needs. In-memory state must be safe to lose: connections, caches, running ffmpeg.
2. Anything a replica does at startup is safe when every replica does it at the same instant. Upserts use one conflict target, and a table must not have a second unique index that can race it.
3. Work that must happen once uses a lease or an advisory lock.
4. Routes belong to one role. Bandwidth-heavy routes go on `stream`.

### Data

5. Item IDs survive renames. A moved file keeps its item and watch state.
6. Filtering and sorting use indexed columns. Response shaping happens in memory from `items.dto`.
7. Client input reaches SQL only as bound parameters. Sort keys come from an allowlist.
8. Migrations are forward-only, and an applied migration is never edited. Dropping a column breaks replicas still running the old code, so after the first release, removals ship one release after the code stops using them.

### Messaging

9. Never call `pg_notify` inside a data transaction. Commit durably, then send the hint in a separate `synchronous_commit=off` transaction. Inline, it capped at about 189 commits/s.
10. Every hint consumer also polls, so a lost hint only adds delay.

### Storage

11. POSIX and S3 behave the same at the `Store` interface. The conformance suite runs against POSIX, SeaweedFS and Garage.
12. S3 credentials come from the environment. Store URIs are persisted and logged, so they never hold secrets: `store.Open` refuses a URI with userinfo or a parameter named like a key, secret, access, token, password, signature or credential, at startup, with an error that names the problem and not the value. The parameters are searched after `;` as well as `&`, in the fragment, and inside the query of a URL-valued parameter such as `endpoint`, plain or percent-encoded. A value with no such name, such as a token in an endpoint's path, can't be told from a gateway's path prefix, so it is accepted and reaches logs like the rest of the URI.
13. ffmpeg reads S3 through the loopback input, never a presigned URL.
14. Direct play is proxied by default, and we always answer HEAD ourselves, because presigned URLs reject it.
15. `http.ServeContent` callers set `Content-Type`, or it reads the object to sniff one.

### Compatibility

16. Routing is case-insensitive and query keys are lowercased once, in middleware. Route wildcards keep their case.
17. `TranscodingUrl` stays Jellyfin-shaped and unsigned. Signing the URLs inside playlists is still to do: today each line carries the client's own token as `ApiKey`, as Jellyfin's do.
18. `PlaySessionId` can be empty in reports, so progress keys on user and item. Coalescing happens in SQL, never in process memory; see invariant 28.
19. `<video>` can't send headers, so media routes accept `ApiKey` in the query.
20. `compat/endpoints.yaml` is generated from `Routes()`, and every alias must be a real obsolete route. Tests enforce both.

### Transcoding

21. Segments are fMP4 by default. MPEG-TS seams break RFC 8216.
22. A segment's video bytes depend on the spec hash and segment number, not on which worker made them. Its audio bytes can differ after a restart, on both paths, because the encoder starts warm and does not continue (invariants 24 and 48); decoded they are the same to within the bound in invariant 48. A copied segment's length follows the layout (invariant 47), as a re-encoded one's is the fixed grid.
23. fMP4 can't splice across encoder families, so failover stays within a family. A copied stream and a re-encoded one are different families: the copy flag is in the spec hash, so their segments never mix.
24. The exact ffmpeg settings in `spikes/seam/README.md` are required, including fixed B-frames and a closed GOP. For x264 that is `bframes=2:b-adapt=0:open-gop=0:scenecut=0`; the seam test in `internal/transcode` fails if a restart drifts from a run from zero. The code leaves out `-to`: a job runs to the end of the file or until it is stopped, because a job's end is decided by who is still watching, not by a time. The cost is one padded AAC frame past the picture at the very end of a run that started late, which the seam test excludes. Frames the decoder marks interlaced go through `yadif` (one frame out per frame in, so timing is untouched) because the output is progressive; `OutputVersion` was bumped for it.
25. Every job has a kill path, and no ffmpeg outlives its lease. The paths are: `Sessions/Playing/Stopped`, `DELETE Videos/ActiveEncodings` (sent twice, so idempotent), and no request for the spec in `transcodeIdle` (60 seconds), because closing a tab sends nothing. A worker that loses its lease, can't renew it, or shuts down kills its ffmpeg, and the kernel does if the worker is killed outright. That includes the extraction ffmpeg of a subtitle track (invariant 43).

### Security

26. Tokens are random and stored per device. Passwords use bcrypt. Login failures don't reveal which usernames exist.
27. Paths built from keys can't leave the store's root.

### Play state and sessions

28. Coalescing reads `user_item_data.progress_at`, which only Progress and Stopped write. Playing, favorite and played toggles never touch it, so they can't suppress a report. The first Progress after Playing is stored, unless a Stopped or Progress landed within the last 30 seconds.
29. Runtime comparisons use `numeric`, and `ActiveWithinSeconds` is clamped to about eight years, so no client value can overflow a bigint or an interval. `PositionTicks` is clamped to `playstate.MaxTicks` so stored positions stay sane.
30. A late Stopped or Progress can't move a session: Stopped clears the row and Progress updates it only when its `item_id` matches. Playing always sets the item, so a late Playing can still move it.
31. A device can hold several sockets. Each is a `session_sockets` row, and `sessions.ws_replica` is the newest row's replica, or NULL. Connect and close update both under the session row's lock, in one transaction. Any frame a socket receives refreshes its row, and detach deletes the device's rows quiet for two minutes, so a crashed replica's row stops being a candidate for the claim once any of that device's sockets next closes. Until then it can still become the claim. A device that never closes a socket again keeps its stale row until Phase 4 cleanup.
32. Item DTOs carry the video, the default audio stream and the text subtitle tracks (subrip, ass, ssa, webvtt, mov_text), so the details page offers no track that can't be delivered. Image tracks (PGS, VobSub, DVB) are listed nowhere, because nothing extracts or draws them yet; a file with only those plays as it did. `media_sources.streams` keeps the full list. A text track in the DTO has `IsTextSubtitleStream` and `SupportsExternalStream` and no `DeliveryUrl`, because the DTO is stored and a URL carries the caller's token. PlaybackInfo lists every audio stream when transcoding is on, because a stream change can then deliver any of them, and gives each text track its delivery, as the capture shows for direct play and transcode alike: `DeliveryMethod: External` with `IsExternalUrl: false` and a relative `DeliveryUrl` of `/Videos/{item}/{source}/Subtitles/{index}/0/Stream.vtt?ApiKey=...` (`.ass` for ass and ssa tracks). The chosen track a client sends is echoed as `DefaultSubtitleStreamIndex` only where its position in `MediaStreams` equals its `Index`, because jellyfin-web reads that field as a position; where an unlisted stream (a second audio track in direct play, an image track) shifts it, the field stays -1 and the track is still in the menu to pick by hand. Menu labels (`DisplayTitle`) read like Jellyfin's, `eng - Default - SUBRIP`, so tracks in one language differ. A source scanned before the scanner recorded which tracks are text lists none until its next scan, which it does without the file changing. No track is listed that can't be delivered: where the reply asks for burn-in but this server can't transcode, it lists no subtitle track, and likewise when a transcode store is configured but no worker is running, since the default then plays on. And with no worker running at all (none with the worker role has reported in 10 seconds), nothing can extract a track, so a text track whose extracted object isn't in the asset store is dropped from the film's details (`GET /Items/{id}` and its alias, both `MediaStreams` lists) and from PlaybackInfo, direct play included; cached tracks stay listed, and tracks return when a worker does. This costs one worker query and one asset-store request for each text track, only for one item's details or PlaybackInfo and only while no worker runs. The check can race with a worker coming back, which only hides a track for a moment. A replica that can't open the asset store (no S3 credentials) or gets no answer from it within 5 seconds lists no text track (it can't tell which are cached) and still answers the request, rather than failing or hanging it. Lists (`/Items` queries, Continue Watching, Latest) are not filtered: they cost a store request for every track of every item, and jellyfin-web offers a track menu only for a single item (details page and player). No list view of jellyfin-web shows these tracks: its card builders read only the video stream, and the track menus and media-info dialog load the item with `getItem`, which is filtered.
33. `Content-Type` for direct play comes from the stored container, never from the URL. Matroska and WebM share a container, so only that case also reads the store key's extension.

### Transcoding, continued

34. A transcode URL's query is the only input to a spec, and every request validates it again, because clients can edit it. A client value reaches ffmpeg only as a bounded integer, never as text, and segment numbers outside the playlist are refused.
35. Segments of a re-encoded stream are 4 seconds, the length the seam spike validated, not the 3 seconds Jellyfin 12.4 uses (`compat/captures/transcode-12.4.md`). Nothing else depends on Jellyfin's length: the client reads durations from the playlist, and the server reads them from the playlist's `Layout` (`playback.Layout`), never from a constant (a segment's bytes are in invariant 22): a copied stream's segments are as long as the source's keyframes make them, up to 12 seconds (invariant 47). For a re-encode the segment length is in the spec hash; a copy's follows the index, which the hash doesn't cover (invariant 47).
36. A playlist is complete VOD with `EXT-X-ENDLIST` and no discontinuity tags, computed from the duration before anything is encoded. A tail under a second joins the segment before it in a re-encoded stream, because the encoder may not cut one; a copied stream's last segment starts where the layout says (invariant 47), not always at the last keyframe, and runs to the end, however short. The duration is where the *video* ends, because the muxer cuts segments only on video and audio that runs longer would otherwise leave playlist entries nothing can produce. A re-encoded stream takes it from the probe (`DurationTicks` on the video stream, else the format duration; Matroska's tag and format duration are ends on the file's clock, so the probe takes the video's start time off them). A copied stream takes it from `media_sources.video_end`, which the scan reads from the packets (invariants 46 and 47), because those durations don't say where the video ends in every case.
37. Any `stream` replica serves segment N: from the transcode store, else by waiting on an active job that will reach it (within three segments of its progress), else by starting a job at N. That check-and-insert runs under a per-spec advisory lock, so concurrent requests start one job. A request replaces the same viewer's previous job, whether it then starts a job or joins another viewer's, unless another viewer's latest wait was on that job (`transcode_viewers.job_id`), which is left running; other viewers' jobs are left alone. The init segment's URL carries no number and any job's first segment brings the init with it, so its request joins a live job, else starts one at the segment the viewer starts at (`StartTimeTicks` is in every segment URL), never at 0 for a resume to replace. A media request for segment N starts its job at N, even below the resume point. A request that cannot be served in time fails with an error rather than hang: 500 with the reason logged if the job failed, 503 once a queued job has waited `queueTimeout` (6 seconds) without a worker taking it, whether none is running or every slot is busy (the log says which). That wait is what lets a worker that is stopping gracefully (not one that is killed) be replaced without the viewer noticing, shown with real processes by `test/roles.sh`: its job is cancelled, the next request queues a new one, and a replacement that arrives within the timeout takes it. jellyfin-web's player abandons a request after 10 seconds, so a refusal must come sooner. 504 on timeout, 410 if the viewer was stopped. The exception to a segment's failure leading to the "cannot be played" dialog before playback is a copied stream whose keyframe index was found wrong: the index is dropped and the next PlaybackInfo re-encodes, with no dialog (invariant 47).
    Before playback has begun, jellyfin-web answers a failed segment by destroying its player and asking PlaybackInfo again, with no limit and no dialog of its own, so a failing stream is a request storm, not an error unless PlaybackInfo refuses (below). After playback has begun it answers a failed segment by dropping the stream: the picture freezes, there is no dialog, and it asks for nothing more. The server can do nothing about that second case from the failed response, so a worker that dies mid-film freezes the viewer's picture, and a replacement within `queueTimeout` does not save it, because the dead job keeps its lease until it expires (a lease lasts 10 seconds from each renewal, which is 7 to 10 seconds after a kill, and up to 30 seconds from the claim until the first heartbeat, about 3 seconds after ffmpeg starts), and only then does the waiting request queue a new job (measured: a segment answered after 12.7 seconds, with the replacement started 1 second after SIGKILL); recovering from that without losing the picture or the resume position is Phase 4 (`PRODUCT.md`). The failover that exists is only this: a job whose worker is gone is cancelled and restarted by the next segment request, and a replacement worker that arrives within `queueTimeout` takes the restarted job, which is only fast enough when the old worker stopped gracefully and cancelled its job. The fix for the first case is at PlaybackInfo: it refuses a transcode that can't start now, by answering `ErrorCode: RateLimitExceeded` with no media sources, which jellyfin-web shows as a "Playback Error" dialog saying the media can't be played at this time (`NoCompatibleStream` would blame the client). It refuses when no worker has reported in 10 seconds (`transcode_nodes`, rows with slots), when every worker slot is taken by jobs of other viewers (the asking device's own jobs don't count: jellyfin-web asks for the new stream before it deletes the old), or when the spec's last job failed within 30 seconds of its end (`transcode_jobs.ended_at`), and a segment request in that time gets the failure too rather than start the same job again. A segment failure before playback therefore ends in that dialog: the PlaybackInfo the player asks again is refused, unless the spec has a live job, which is offered (below). The exception is a copied segment that fails because the keyframe index is wrong: the index is dropped, the copy is no longer offered, and the next PlaybackInfo re-encodes with no dialog (invariant 47). A spec that already has a live job is always offered, by every replica, also one with no `transcodeStore`, since a viewer joins it, whatever failed lately and whatever the stores say; if the stores differ, the log warns and names both, and what the viewer then gets depends on the replica that serves the segments. One with no `transcodeStore` answers 503 at once, which sends jellyfin-web back to PlaybackInfo, where the live job is offered again, so before playback the viewer retries with no dialog until the job ends, which `transcodeIdle` bounds only if nobody else is watching it; after playback has begun the 503 freezes the picture as above. One with a store it can't read the segments in waits for them, keeps the job watched, and its request times out. A queued job is live for `queueTimeout` (6 seconds); whoever sees an older one cancels it and workers don't claim it, so a returning worker starts no ffmpeg for a viewer who left. With no live job on the spec, it also refuses when the live `transcode_nodes` rows differ in `transcodeStore`. Every process that takes part, whatever its roles, registers its roles, store URI and (for a worker) slots there with a heartbeat, and a process with no store registers an empty one, so a missing store and a different one are found by the same comparison; the log names both roles and both stores. The one process left out is a worker-only process with no store: it extracts subtitles and runs no transcodes, so it shares no segments with anyone. Nothing else reports a configuration fault. When the viewer's default track plays as it is, a pick of another audio track that can't be transcoded now plays the default instead of refusing, whatever codec the pick has, though the client's menu still shows the track it asked for; when the default track needs the transcode, PlaybackInfo refuses. The one case the comparison cannot see is the same URI on different volumes: a job that ends having stored a segment this replica cannot read is marked failed with that cause, and PlaybackInfo then refuses with it for 30 seconds. A stream replica with no `transcodeStore` answers a transcode request with 503.
38. A job lives while a viewer on its spec who hasn't been stopped has asked for something in the last `transcodeIdle`. Stopping a viewer marks it stopped rather than deleting it, so a request still in flight can't bring it back (`Segment` checks before it starts a job, and answers 410), and cancels at once every job no unstopped viewer is left watching (a viewer watches the job it last waited on, or any of the spec's if it has only read stored segments), so their slots are free for the stream that replaces them. A job whose ffmpeg finishes no segment in 30 seconds (`Worker.Stall`) is failed: the lease is renewed whatever ffmpeg does, and viewers would otherwise wait on it for good. A Stopped report with no `PlaySessionId` stops the device's viewers of that item (invariant 18). Two viewers on one spec share segments and jobs, and stopping one leaves the other's. A job cut short because its worker shut down or died (its lease ran out and a sweep found it), or lost to a seek, is `cancelled`: viewers restart it, and the spec is not refused. Only a job that ran and broke is `failed`.
39. Segments of a spec nobody has asked for in `transcodeRetention` (24 hours) are deleted by a worker's sweep.
40. A segment the encoder flushes when its input ends is cut wherever the input stopped, and ffmpeg exits 0 either way, so the worker stores that last segment only if it is the playlist's last one and its length is within 1.5 seconds of the playlist's (the playlist's own length for it, which is the index's for a copied stream). A job whose input ended early otherwise fails with "the input ended early". `Reader` resumes a cut S3 body (invariant 42), but whether ffmpeg's own reads are exposed to the cut is unproven; this check remains for a source file that is itself cut short. It judges the last segment's length, so a source whose audio alone ends early is not caught; that stays open.
41. A re-encode never exceeds 1080 lines (`playback.MaxHeight`; the deinterlacing of interlaced video is a re-encode's too), whatever the source and the cap, because software x264 at 4K needs more CPU than a worker usually has and the player stalls. Size is in the spec hash, so segments of different sizes never mix. A copy has no such limit, since it costs almost nothing; it is the client's `CodecProfiles` (resolution, level) that decide whether the picture may be sent as it is.
42. An S3 read is one open-ended GET with the caller's context, so cancelling the caller ends the transfer. SeaweedFS can end the body of a GET early, with a clean EOF and no Content-Length error, when a neighbouring GET of the same object is dropped while it is being read, and can answer a resumed range GET with `200`, `Content-Length: 0` and no `Content-Range`; Garage never cut a body in the same tests; whether it can answer that way was not tested. ffmpeg drops a request whenever it seeks. So `S3.Open` turns a body that ends before its Content-Length into `io.ErrUnexpectedEOF`, and a GET sent with a Range that comes back without a `Content-Range` is not that range and reads as `io.ErrUnexpectedEOF` whatever its body; `store.Reader` resumes at its current offset on that error, and also when a known-size object ends cleanly short of its size. Segments are read through a `Reader` of unknown size (`math.MaxInt64`), which cannot tell a clean short end, so `readSegment` treats an empty segment (never legitimate), or one still cut once its `Reader` gives up, as not there yet, and the wait loop reads again. A `Reader` gives up after three reopens without progress. Evidence: on `chrislusf/seaweedfs:latest` (weed 4.50, image created 2026-10-25, not pinned) a probe of raw GETs held while neighbours were dropped found 67 of 200 bodies cut, at 256 KiB multiples, and 0 of 200 on Garage; that probe is not in the tree. `TestDroppedReadDoesNotCutAnother` (100 rounds) drops three neighbours during a held `Reader` read and fails on SeaweedFS with the resume removed (10 of 10 runs, `unexpected EOF`), and passes with it on SeaweedFS and Garage (10 of 10). The cases of an empty 200, a clean short body, an empty segment and a segment that stays cut have tests that fail with each fix removed. No test in the tree shows the cut harming real ffmpeg: a repro of 30 encodes per target kept every frame with the resume on and also with it off, so ffmpeg's own exposure is unproven, and an earlier loopback-input run that lost its input in 17 of 80 encodes was not reproduced. `checkLast` (invariant 40) remains for a source that is itself cut short.
43. A text subtitle track is read out of its file once, whatever the number of requests and replicas, by a worker: only workers run media tools (ARCHITECTURE rule 1), so `api` and `stream` run no ffmpeg or ffprobe, and `test/roles.sh` and `test/e2e/run.sh` run them with none on their PATH. The first request for an uncached track (`transcode.FetchSubtitle`) inserts a row in `subtitle_jobs`, keyed by the object it will become, `subtitles/{source}/{hash of the ETag}/{index}.json` in the library's asset store, with `ON CONFLICT` replacing only a done or failed row older than 30 seconds, so one live row exists per track however many requests, replicas and burn-in jobs ask. It is a table of its own, not rows of `transcode_jobs`, because an extraction has no spec, viewer or owner and must not count against transcode slots, `Unavailable` or the queue timeout. A worker claims it with `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` (`Worker.RunSubtitles`), runs one ffmpeg into every format (vtt, srt, ass; js is made from the vtt) through `store.Input` so S3 sources need no credentials in argv, and puts the result in the asset store as one object, so a reader never sees half of it. The requests wait on the asset store, which is the result, and on the row (every 500 ms and on the `subtitle` hint, which transcode claimers don't listen for). A worker runs at most four extractions at once on a semaphore of its own, never the transcode slots, so an extraction proceeds while every transcode slot is busy, which matters because jellyfin-web fetches a subtitle once and doesn't retry. A burn-in job asks for its track the same way and waits holding its transcode slot, as it did; the extraction itself runs on a subtitle slot, of this worker or another. The row has a lease the worker renews, and a worker killed mid-extraction (ffmpeg dies with it) loses it: after at most 10 seconds another worker claims the row again, with `attempts` incremented on each claim; a lease lost after the third claim fails the row instead. An ordinary ffmpeg failure (a file that is garbage or gone) fails the row at once, without retry, with the error text. A failed or done row is replaced only after 30 seconds (`failureMemory`), so within that window every request for a track that couldn't be read gets the 500 "the subtitle track could not be read" at once, logged with the item, source, index and the worker's error, and the file isn't read again; later the next request queues it afresh. A request waits as long as a live worker is on the job (a claimed row with a lease that is renewed), which for a big file on S3 is as long as reading it takes, up to the extraction's own limit of an hour (`subtitles.DefaultTimeout`, named in the error) and two hours in all; a worker that is stopped gracefully puts its job back in the queue. With no worker running (no `transcode_nodes` row with the worker role seen in the last 10 seconds), or a row that stays unclaimed (queued, or its lease lapsed) while no extraction is running anywhere, for six seconds (`queueTimeout`, the same wait as a segment's) the request answers 503 and the row stays queued for the worker that returns; so no request hangs. That covers a worker from before subtitle jobs, which reports in as a worker but never claims them. A row queued behind busy workers isn't refused: an extraction holding a live lease shows jobs are being claimed, so the request or burn-in job waits its turn, because jellyfin-web asks once and a refusal would lose the track. A worker cut off from the database stops its extraction on a timer that only a successful renewal puts off, one heartbeat before the lease can lapse however its renewals fail, so a partition doesn't read the file twice; the job is then taken over like a killed worker's. Waiters read the asset store only once the row says done, so a long extraction costs each a query every 500 ms and no store requests. Uncached tracks are listed whenever a worker reports in, so during an upgrade the workers must be replaced before `stream` and `api`. A cached track is read from the asset store with no job and no worker. Only a text track of the source, an index in `media_sources.streams` that the probe marked, is served; any other index is 404, as is a format other than vtt, srt, js or ass, including none. A non-zero `startPositionTicks` moves every cue earlier by that much and stops at zero, as the capture shows; ASS keeps its styles. Times are counted from the source's start time, as ffmpeg extracts them. Start and end of an extraction are logged at Info with the item, source, index, file size and duration. Extraction gives cues on the viewer's clock, which starts at zero, on ffmpeg 8.1 and 9.0; ffmpeg 6.1, which CI installs, also applies a negative start such as AAC's, so cues there can be 23 ms late, which burn-in cancels and nobody can see. ffmpeg runs with `-v error`, and any output at all, which is how it reports a file cut short while exiting 0, fails the extraction and caches nothing. jellyfin-web makes one subtitle fetch and passes no timeout to it: the bundle's `fetchWithTimeout` applies only to callers that give one, and neither the captures nor the code name a limit; Chromium held such a fetch for 200 seconds and then took the answer, so only a proxy in front bounds the first extraction in a big file, and the extraction continues for the next request to find. The only related figure is Jellyfin's server-side `SubtitleExtractionTimeoutMinutes: 30` (`compat/captures/transcode-12.4.json`). The 503 for no worker is what the `test/roles.sh` check with real processes measures, under ten seconds. After a client-rendered subtitle is picked in a transcode, switching audio leaves jellyfin-web's track disabled with no cues until re-picked; real Jellyfin 12.4 does the same, so it is a client limitation and the server does nothing about it.
44. A subtitle is burnt into the picture only when it can't be shown any other way: the client's DeviceProfile has no `SubtitleProfiles` (jellyfin-web sends none when the viewer's setting is to burn all), or the viewer has chosen to always burn in when transcoding and the film is transcoded anyway. Then PlaybackInfo marks every text track `DeliveryMethod: Encode` with no `DeliveryUrl`, a chosen text track goes in the TranscodingUrl as `SubtitleStreamIndex`, in `Params` and in the spec hash, and `SubtitleCodecNotSupported` joins the reasons; with no `SubtitleProfiles`, that reason alone forces a transcode, even at Auto bitrate. A client that shows subtitles itself is told nothing of them in the TranscodingUrl, and choosing one causes no new PlaybackInfo, no job and no playlist change. Another burnt track, or none, is another spec, replaced like an audio switch: PlaybackInfo, `DELETE ActiveEncodings`, a new master. The worker draws the track as ASS from a file in its job directory after the scale, with the cue times moved later by the source's start time because `-copyts` keeps frames in the source's own clock and extraction does not; a restart at any segment therefore draws the same text at the same moments (`TestBurnedSubtitleIsAtTheRightTime` restarts at segments 3, 6 and 9, a source with a 7.5 s start included, and fails without the shift). No client text reaches ffmpeg's arguments: the index is an integer (invariant 34), the file name is fixed. The job's first start for a track waits for the extraction (invariant 43), which another worker or this one makes through a subtitle job, shared with every request for the track, before ffmpeg starts, and its scratch directory is made after the read, so the sweep of idle directories can't remove it, and the worker renews the job's lease meanwhile, so a long extraction is not swept; it stops waiting, and frees its slot, if the job is lost or no viewer is watching. The extraction continues for the others. The viewer's player gives up on a segment after 10 seconds and asks again, so the first burn of a track in a big file shows a still picture until the read ends; this was not measured on a big file, only the small fixtures were. When the worker slots are all taken and the default track plays as it is, a chosen burnt-in subtitle is dropped with the audio pick (invariant 37).

### Video copy

45. A film's video is copied, not re-encoded, when the client plays it as it is and only the audio or the container forced the stream. "Plays it" means the video is allowed in the client's transcoding profile, so a copy is also chosen when its direct-play profile rejected the codec (an MP4 container it lists no H.264 for, say) but the transcoding profile takes H.264: the video is H.264 and passes the client's `CodecProfiles` for it (profile, level, range type, interlacing, resolution; `Source.CanCopy`, `Decide`), the bitrate cap is not below the file's (with a cap and a file of unknown bitrate, the video is re-encoded; at Auto, which is no cap, a copy carries the file's whole bitrate and size, 4K included, exactly as direct play of the same file does and as Jellyfin's copy does, so a viewer on a slow link picks a cap, which re-encodes), no subtitle is burnt in, and the source has a keyframe index (invariant 46) whose keyframes can be cut into segments within `MaxCopySegment` (invariant 47). Everything else is re-encoded as before. That 4K passes at Auto follows from the code (`CanCopy` reads width and height only from a profile condition, and caps nothing at Auto) and from the captured profile, whose H.264 conditions are profile, range type, level 52 and interlacing, with no width or height (`compat/captures/transcode-12.4.json`); capture scenario 3 is a 720p film and says nothing of 4K. The reasons are the ones a re-encode gets (`AudioCodecNotSupported`, `ContainerNotSupported`, ...), as captured, and the PlaybackInfo reply has the same fields; only the URL differs, in `VideoCodec=copy` where a re-encode says `h264`. Like every query value that reaches the spec (invariant 34), `copy` is validated again on every request: `NewSpec` refuses it for a source that can't be copied, and for one that also names a burnt subtitle track, so a client that edits the URL gets 400 and not a broken stream. `Copy` is in the spec hash, so copied and re-encoded segments never mix, but a cap and the bitrate are not: a copy has the source's own size, and a bitrate estimated from its file for `BANDWIDTH`, which a cap doesn't change. The master playlist's `CODECS` names the source's own H.264 profile and level.
46. The keyframe index is read at scan time, by `library.ReadKeyframes`, from the file's own index, and stored with the media source in `media_sources.keyframes` (`bigint[]`, in ticks of the file's clock), so every replica and worker agrees and nothing is read again. For Matroska it is the Cues of the first video track, found through the SeekHead; for MP4 it is the sync sample table with the decode and composition times and one edit list. It is read only for H.264 in mkv and mp4. NULL means not looked at, and a scan reads it, which is how files scanned before it get one; empty means the file has no index that can be read (Matroska without Cues, or with Cues its SeekHead doesn't point to, fragmented MP4, an edit list of more than one edit apart from an empty one before it, tables that disagree with each other), and the file is re-encoded without a scan trying again. The scan logs that at Info with the reason, so an operator can see why a film is unusable for a copy; the other reasons a film is re-encoded are in its `TranscodeReasons`. A film whose audio outlasts its video by enough to fail the 12 second check against the file's duration (the last keyframe more than 12 seconds before the end) is re-encoded: an accepted limit, because the check is against the file's duration, which is the audio's end when the audio is longer, and the scan doesn't read the video's end to learn better. Postgres cost: every request for a copied stream's master playlist, playlist or segment reads the keyframe array and `video_end` from `media_sources`, since the URL names the copy (about 1,800 bigints, 14 KB, for a 2 hour film with a keyframe every 4 seconds; the request rate has not been measured). A read that fails for another reason, including the failed read of a Cues or moov body, leaves NULL and is logged as a warning, and the next scan tries again. The tables are walked by runs, not by sample, and refused unless the sync samples ascend and lie within the file's sample count (`stsz`), so a crafted file ends quickly as a file with no index. The scan also stores `media_sources.video_end`, where the last video frame ends on the same clock, from `library.ReadVideoEnd`: a bounded `ffprobe` packet read from the last keyframe to the end of the file, for Matroska and MP4 alike and only when the last keyframe is within 12 seconds of the file's duration (a film with a longer gap can't be cut, and is stored with an empty index, logged, without the read), because the container's durations and tags don't say where the video ends (invariant 47). It opens the start of the file (a few MB; how many depends on the ffmpeg version) and asks for that last stretch twice, so it reads up to twice the distance from the last keyframe to the end, at most 12 seconds of film (120 MB at 40 Mbps); on SeaweedFS and Garage `TestVideoEndFromS3ReadsLittle` bounds the read to 12 MiB plus twice the bytes from the last keyframe to the end (the fixed part varies with the ffmpeg version; the last stretch is what carries over to a feature film). The tests bound `ReadKeyframes` to 2% of a small file from disk (`TestKeyframesReadLittle`) and 5% from SeaweedFS and Garage (`TestKeyframesFromS3ReadLittle`). The Matroska index is the Cues, one entry for each keyframe. The MP4's is the moov, which has a table entry for every frame and audio packet, so a large MP4's moov read is megabytes and grows with the length of the film, not with its bitrate. Listing the keyframes with `ffprobe` instead reads all of the file. Each read asks for the bytes it needs and closes (an open-ended S3 GET closed early may deliver a little more before it stops). `TestKeyframesMatchPackets` compares the index with `ffprobe` on every packet of the same files, with moov at the end or in front, an edit list or none, and the video as the second track.
47. A copied stream is cut on the keyframes (`playback.CopyLayout`): a segment ends at the first keyframe at least 4 seconds after the one it began at, or, if that would make it longer than `MaxCopySegment` (12 seconds), at the last keyframe before. The last ends where the video does. Times count from the first keyframe, and the video lasts from it to `video_end` (invariant 46), which is where the last frame ends, read from the packets, not guessed from the container's duration or tags: those count from the file's first stream, which may start before the video, and can be the audio's. The last entry of the playlist is within one frame of the real last segment when the video starts late, the audio starts late, everything does, or the video is shorter than the audio, in Matroska and in MP4 (`TestCopiedPlaylistEndsWhereTheVideoDoes`, `TestVideoEndIsWhereTheLastFrameEnds`). With a keyframe every 2 seconds that is 4 second segments, as a re-encode has; with a longer gap a segment is as long as the gap, so `EXTINF` is the real length (Jellyfin 12.4 writes fifteen 6.000 s entries for an MP4 whose segments are really 7, 6, 5, 8 ... s, `compat/captures/transcode-12.4.md`; for Matroska it computes them as we do), `EXT-X-TARGETDURATION` is the longest rounded up, and there is no discontinuity tag. No segment is longer than `MaxCopySegment`, so a file is re-encoded only when two keyframes, or the last keyframe and the end of the video, are more than 12 seconds apart. The longest segment can be longer than the longest gap, since two short gaps make one segment (keyframes every 3.75 seconds give 7.5 second segments). A client must download a whole segment before it shows any of it, so a long one delays every seek and start by its size at the client's bandwidth, which for 12 seconds at 40 Mbps is 60 MB. It lets through x264's and x265's default of a keyframe every 250 frames at 21 fps and up (10.4 s at 24 fps), with scene cuts between (`TestCopyLayoutKeepsSegmentsWithinTheLimit`). The playlist, `At` (the segment holding a resume position) and the worker's `checkLast` all read the same `Layout`, never a constant 4. Cues and stss are seek indexes and may not match the fragments ffmpeg cuts. When the gatherer finds a segment that doesn't start or end on a keyframe of the file (`errWrongIndex`), the job fails, the worker sets the source's keyframes to an empty array, only if the file's ETag is still the one the job started with (a file rescanned meanwhile keeps its new index), and logs an error with the item and the source, and the next PlaybackInfo re-encodes. An empty array stays until the file changes; an operator who wants the scan to read it again sets `keyframes` to NULL on that `media_sources` row. A last segment with no fragments is told apart by where ffmpeg's output stopped. Fragments are cut at every real keyframe, so with a good index none runs past the last segment's start: if the output stopped at or before it, the input was cut short, as when a faststart MP4 stops at the byte its last keyframe starts, whatever the length of the last group of frames, and the index is kept; if the output ran past it, the last entry is false and the index is dropped (`TestCopiedMP4CutAtTheLastKeyframeKeepsTheIndex`, `TestCopiedMP4CutAtAShortLastGroupKeepsTheIndex`, `TestCopiedStreamWithAFalseLastKeyframeIsDropped`); jellyfin-web asks PlaybackInfo again when a segment fails before playback. Only that error does this, never a transient failure or a cancellation (`TestCopiedStreamWithAWrongIndexFailsAndIsReEncoded`, and the early-end and stop tests, which check the index is left alone); a copy URL for that source is then refused as in invariant 45. The spec hash doesn't cover the index, so a change to the layout rules (`CopyLayout`, `MaxCopySegment`) or to the parser that gives other keyframes for the same file needs `OutputVersion` bumped, or segments stored under the old layout would be served for the new playlist. A viewer already playing a copy whose index is found wrong gets 400 on every request for the copy, stored segments included, because `NewSpec` refuses the copy (invariant 45), and jellyfin-web freezes with no dialog. That can be long before the bad point, since the job reads ahead of the viewer at 5 times the film's speed and finds it first. It is an accepted gap, listed in `PRODUCT.md` beside the Phase 4 mid-film freeze, until the job's read-ahead has a limit.
48. A copied job (`CopyCommand`, `gatherer`) copies the video and re-encodes the audio with the settings of a re-encode, writes one fragment for each keyframe of the source (`-hls_time 0.001`: the muxer cuts at the first keyframe after hls_time times the segments written, counted from its own first packet, so with irregular keyframes a run begun elsewhere would cut elsewhere, `spikes/seam/README.md`), and joins fragments into the playlist's segments as they finish. A segment's video bytes depend only on the file and the segment's number, and its audio is timed the same way, but the audio's bytes may differ after a restart, because the encoder starts warm and does not continue: the fragments are told apart by their times, read from the playlist ffmpeg writes and from the first fragment's first keyframe, the join drops each fragment's `styp` and sets its sequence number to its start time, and a segment that doesn't start and end on fragment boundaries fails the job rather than store the wrong video. The run for segment N begins at the keyframe before it, and its audio 2 seconds early on the AAC grid of a run from the start, so the audio is warm and whole at N and nothing depends on the decode time of a keyframe that Matroska doesn't store. Restarts at segments 1, 4, 8 and the last give the same video bytes, the same init segment and audio packets at the same times as a run from the start, on ffmpeg 9.0.2, 6.1.1 and 8.1.3 (`TestCopiedRestartSplicesWithEarlierSegments`); decoded, the audio differs from a run from the start by at most 0.31% to 0.36% of full scale (about -49 dB, depending on the ffmpeg version) on the fixture's 440 Hz tone, which the test bounds at 1%. Stopping, DELETE ActiveEncodings, a closed tab, a second viewer, an audio switch, a far seek and S3 sources use the machinery of a re-encode and are exercised on copied streams by `test/e2e` and `TestCopied*`. A job whose input ends early fails with "the input ended early", except when the cut is within 1.5 seconds of the end of the film, inside the last segment: the job then succeeds and that segment is short by that much. That is an accepted limit, because a tighter check would fail good files whose last fragment ends early (118 to 159 ms measured). A copy costs about 5% to 8% of the CPU of the re-encode of the same 1080p film at a 3 Mbps cap (1.08 against 19.89 CPU seconds of the worker's ffmpeg for 32 seconds of film in the latest run, 0.90 against 11.22 in an earlier one on the same machine, measured by `test/e2e` from the worker's children, with the AC3 decode and AAC encode included). A copy opens the file twice, once for the video and once for the audio, and ffmpeg reads each at 5 times the film's speed (`-readrate 5`, `TestCopyReadsAtFiveTimesTheFilmsSpeed`), so while the job runs it reads ten times the film's bitrate, and a run to the end reads twice the film's bytes. That keeps a copy from reading, writing and uploading the whole film as fast as the disk and the store allow, but it does not follow the viewer: there is no limit on how far ahead of the viewer it gets. It continues until the film ends or 60 seconds after the last request (`transcodeIdle`). For a 2 hour film at 40 Mbps (36 GB) a run to the end reads 72 GB from the source, which from S3 is 72 GB of egress, writes and uploads 36 GB of segments that are kept for 24 hours, and takes about 24 minutes; a viewer who leaves after a minute still costs the minute watched and the 60 seconds of idle time, 120 seconds of the job running at ten times the film's bitrate: 6 GB at 40 Mbps (5 MB/s, 50 MB/s read). The byte figures here are computed from the bitrate and the length, not measured by a test. They assume the store takes uploads at 5 times the film's speed (200 Mbps for a 40 Mbps film), and ffmpeg is not paced by uploads: a slower store leaves what ffmpeg has made in the scratch directory, up to the whole 36 GB, and it holds only what was made and not yet uploaded when the store keeps up. A copied seek waits for the whole segment it lands in, read at 5 times the film's speed and downloaded at the file's bitrate: up to 12 seconds of film, 60 MB at 40 Mbps. Resuming a 1080p film at 40 s of 60 s, which starts a job there as a seek past what is made does, took 2.6 s from the click to the picture moving on POSIX (2614 ms) and on SeaweedFS (2628 ms); a segment needs the film read up to its end at 5 times its speed, so the first of 4 seconds takes under a second and one of 12 seconds under 3. A copied stream's storage is the file's own bitrate, not the encoder's ceiling (`Spec.VideoBitrate` is `FileBitrate`), kept for 24 hours; the storage ceilings of a re-encode do not apply to it.
