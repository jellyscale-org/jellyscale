# Transcoding

Add `transcodeStore` to the config, the same for every role. With none anywhere, nothing transcodes and every source plays as it is. So that a viewer is never given a track other than the one they pick, a browser's audio menu then offers one track, the file's default (D-029); the same holds for a film with no probed duration, with no live worker that runs transcodes (none alive, or the only one has no cores) and while the roles' stores differ. Every process records its roles and `transcodeStore` in the database with a heartbeat, and a replica compares them: when they differ, or one process has none (a worker-only process with none is left out, since it only extracts subtitles), an `api` replica refuses the transcode at PlaybackInfo and its log names both. A spec that already has a live job is offered anyway, by any replica, and its viewer joins it; the log warns and names both stores, and if the replica that serves the segments has no `transcodeStore`, it answers 503 and jellyfin-web asks PlaybackInfo again, which offers the live job again, so before playback the viewer retries without a dialog until the job ends, and after playback has begun the picture freezes; if it has a store it can't read the segments in, the request times out. A store URI that carries credentials (a user or password, or a parameter named like a key, secret, access, token, pass, sig or credential) is refused at startup; credentials come from the environment. A process with only the `worker` role and no store starts and extracts subtitles, but runs no transcodes. Set it, to the same value, on all of them:

| Key | Meaning | Default |
|---|---|---|
| `transcodeStore` | Store URI for segments: `file:///var/lib/jellyscale/segments`, or `s3://bucket/segments?endpoint=...` with credentials from the environment | none |
| `transcodeScratch` | Where a worker's ffmpeg writes before uploading. Workers on one host may share it; each sweep removes job directories untouched for two minutes, which a crash leaves behind | `$TMPDIR/jellyscale-transcode` |
| `transcodeCores` | The cores a worker gives its transcode jobs, each of which costs some of them (the table under Sizing a worker), paused ones included, apart from up to four subtitle extractions; a negative number is refused at startup. `transcodeSlots`, which it replaces, is still read, when transcodeCores is not set, as that many cores | the processors the process may use, and never fewer than 3.2 (one 4K tone mapped re-encode) |
| `transcodeIdle` | How long without a request before a spec's ffmpeg stops. Closing a tab sends no stop | 60s |
| `transcodeLead` | How much film, from the furthest segment any viewer of a spec has asked for, a transcode job may make before its ffmpeg pauses (below). It is independent of `transcodeIdle`, which still ends a job nobody requests | 60s |
| `transcodeRetention` | How long a spec's segments stay after its last request | 24h |
| `transcodeBudget` | The most the transcode store may hold: a whole number of bytes, or a number with a decimal unit (`KB`, `MB`, `GB`, `TB`; `200GB` is 200 × 10^9 bytes). Over it, a worker's sweep removes whole specs, least recently requested first, but never one a viewer is watching. The age sweep (`transcodeRetention`) still runs beside it. A value that is not a size, or is not positive, stops the start, whatever the role. How to choose it: docs/running.md, Sizing the transcode store | none (no budget: only `transcodeRetention` removes segments) |

`stream` replicas and workers share the segments only through that store and Postgres, so the store must be reachable from both: a shared volume, or S3. The worker needs `ffmpeg` and `ffprobe` on its path, as the image has; `api` and `stream` need neither. Segments of a re-encode are 4 seconds, and a restart after a seek splices with the segments before it.

A viewer whose transcode can't start (no worker running, no worker with the cores the job costs free, or the same one just failed) gets a "cannot be played at this time" dialog from jellyfin-web instead of waiting, answered within seconds; the server log says which. A segment that fails before playback has begun ends in that dialog too, because jellyfin-web asks PlaybackInfo again and is refused. There are two exceptions: a spec with a live job, which is offered again, and a copy whose keyframe index was found wrong, which is dropped so that the next PlaybackInfo re-encodes the film with no dialog. After playback has begun, a failed segment makes jellyfin-web drop the stream: the picture freezes, with no dialog. A worker that stops gracefully and is replaced within six seconds is not noticed, because a request for a segment waits that long for a worker (`test/roles.sh` does this with real processes). A worker that is killed mid-film is replaced without the viewer losing the picture (below). A viewer who picks an audio track that is neither the file's default nor the one their saved language chooses, while no worker has the cores free or that transcode just failed, gets the same dialog and never another track (D-029); their saved language, which they did not pick, falls back to the file's default and plays. In a re-encode, interlaced H.264 is deinterlaced, and the output is H.264 at no more than 1080 lines, in software with `veryfast`; a copy does neither (see Video copy). A worker that falls behind makes the player stall, and there is no lower-quality fallback yet. A job whose ffmpeg makes no segment for 30 seconds is failed, so a hung encode ends in a dialog and not a spinner when it hangs before playback begins; after playback has begun the failed segment freezes the picture as above. A source scanned before the scanner recorded level, field order and range plays directly where its conditions allow it, and is probed again at the next scan, which it does without the file changing.

Storage: segments are kept for `transcodeRetention` (24 hours) after a spec's last request, one set per spec, so a film watched in full at a quality cap of *B* bits per second takes up to *B* × 3600 / 8 bytes per hour of film: about 0.45 GB per hour for each megabit of the cap. The encoder also caps video at 6 × width × height bits per second, and the width follows the source's shape: at 1080 lines a 16:9 spec stays under about 12.63 Mbps (about 5.7 GB per hour) and a 2.39:1 one, 2578 wide, under about 16.9 Mbps (about 7.6 GB per hour); at 720 lines 16:9 stays under about 5.72 Mbps (about 2.6 GB per hour). These are ceilings for a re-encode, with the 192 kbps audio included; none of them applies to a copied stream (see Video copy); how far real film at crf 23 falls below them has not been measured. Each distinct audio track or quality is its own spec. Workers also hold a job's unuploaded segments in `transcodeScratch`, a few at a time.

## Worker failover

What an operator has to do:

- Run at least two workers, with the cores of the dearest film to spare between them, so one can take over the other's jobs.
- Give workers a direct, session-mode Postgres URL, not one behind a transaction-mode pooler (Connections, below).
- Optionally set Postgres's `tcp_keepalives_*`, so that a worker whose host vanishes is noticed in seconds rather than at its 10 second lease (The bounds, below).

A worker killed in the middle of a film is replaced: another worker continues the job at the first missing segment, and jellyfin-web keeps playing, with no dialog. Killed 1.6 seconds into playback (the least buffer there is), the picture of a re-encode stood still for 2.5 to 2.8 seconds in jellyfin-web, and a copy's for 0.3 (`test/e2e`, four runs); a copy's replacement started about 5 seconds after the kill, when the player next asked, because a copy had read ahead and its player had enough to show meanwhile; it goes on by itself, because hls.js abandons a request after 10 seconds, and later in a film, with a full buffer, the viewer sees nothing. This needs a second worker with the job's cores free (or the same one restarted within 6 seconds, as before). With one worker, or none free, the job waits in the queue, and after 6 seconds the segment request fails and the picture freezes with no dialog; only the viewer's next PlaybackInfo, after a seek or a new film, gets the "cannot be played" dialog. Replacement segments splice with the earlier ones (a restart after a seek does the same).

What an operator sees: each worker holds a Postgres session advisory lock for as long as it lives. Every live worker looks at its own row four times a second, and there is one reaper, one worker at a time, that looks for workers whose lock is gone. The reaper is chosen by a one-row lease of 2.5 seconds (`reaperLease`): a worker that is fit to forget others (its own lock held and not lost in the last 10 seconds) takes the role, in one statement, when it is free or lapsed, so that when several ask at once one gets it, and the reaper renews it with each look. A reaper that is killed, stopped (SIGSTOP), too slow to answer its looks or without a lock it can trust stops renewing, and another worker takes the role within 2.5 seconds; a killed reaper is forgotten, like any worker, by the one that replaces it. Reaping costs Postgres the reaper's two `pg_locks` statements four times a second however many workers there are, and each worker's one read of its own row: with 50 workers 8 statements and 200 reads a second, with 400 workers 8 and 1,600 (every worker reaping was 400 and 3,200 statements on `pg_locks`). The reaper, finding a node's lock gone in two looks half a second apart, while holding its own lock and not having lost it in the last 10 seconds, forgets it and gives its jobs half a second to end, its running subtitle extractions and chapter pictures included; a replica forgets nobody, but its PlaybackInfo does not count a worker whose lock is gone, so with the last worker killed a new viewer is refused 1.0 to 1.5 seconds after the kill. After a worker forgets one, a viewer's next request queues the job again for a live worker. Measured with real processes and ffmpeg at twice the film's speed, a SIGKILLed worker's replacement had the first missing segment 4.0 to 4.3 seconds after the kill (2 seconds before the new job starts, then the time ffmpeg takes to make a segment, which a re-encode slower than real time makes longer). Without this it was 13 seconds, past the 10 seconds after which hls.js abandons a request. The logs of one failover: whoever found the dead worker says `workers whose liveness lock is gone were forgotten`, naming the workers and the jobs it cut short; the new job is a `transcode started` for the same spec, at the first missing segment. A worker that lost its lock while it lives says `the worker lost its liveness lock`, after a line with the cause (`the worker's liveness connection ended` with its error, `the liveness connection is open but Postgres does not show its lock`, or `another replica took this worker for dead`), and its jobs end as `transcode interrupted` with `the worker's liveness lock was lost`, or `the worker was taken for dead`; `the worker holds its liveness lock again` says it is back.

The bounds: the lock is dropped by Postgres when the connection closes, which the kernel does however the worker's process dies, so a killed process, an out-of-memory kill, a crash and a stop of the container are all noticed within about a second and a half. A host that loses power, a kernel panic, a network partition, and a pooler or proxy that keeps the server's end of the connection open when the worker's goes away close nothing, and then the job is taken over when its lease runs out, up to 10 seconds after the worker's last renewal, as before, and the dead worker counts as a worker for 10 seconds. Setting Postgres's `tcp_keepalives_idle`, `tcp_keepalives_interval` and `tcp_keepalives_count` makes the server drop such a connection, and the lock, sooner; the job is then taken over a second after, and a worker that is alive but cut off from Postgres may run its ffmpeg until its lease keeper gives the job up, half a second before the lease can lapse (9.5 seconds after its last renewal at the defaults). It cannot store a segment meanwhile: the check before every put is a renewal of the job's lease, sent after the ask, and a job whose lease Postgres does not renew is given up, while a database that does not answer counts as no. The same holds for a worker stopped (SIGSTOP, a frozen VM) for longer than its lease: its job is taken over, and when it goes on it stores nothing. A worker stopped until no later than 9 seconds after its last renewal keeps its jobs. A worker stopped for less than the lease, or a job waiting at its read-ahead limit, keeps its connection and is not taken from. A worker that loses its lock connection while it lives (Postgres restarted, a backend terminated, a proxy dropped an idle connection) goes on with its jobs, claims nothing, takes the lock again (every 50 ms) and then looks for its row; it stops its ffmpeg only if the row is gone, so a job is never stored by two workers. A lock that is gone while the worker's connection stands (the server's end was recycled by a pooler) is found by the worker four times a second, within the half second its jobs are given: the reaper finds its lock gone and forgets it (or, if it is the reaper, it finds its own lock gone in its own look), and the worker finds its row gone within a look, and that is a loss too, logged with a hint to check for a pooler. A Postgres restart or failover ends every worker's lock connection at once; the transcodes go on, the workers lock again and none is restarted. Two node ids that hash alike share a lock (about one chance in four billion for a pair): a dead one looks alive until its lease runs out, and the second of two live ones claims nothing until the first lets go, logging `the liveness lock is held by another node whose id has the same hash` every second; a restart gives it a new id.

Connections: each worker process opens two more Postgres connections beside the pool: one for the lock and one for its lease keeper, which renews every job's lease on a connection that a slow query of the pool's cannot close, and is held while the worker has a job. A fleet of N workers uses 2N direct connections, which no pooler counts, and `api` and `stream` processes use none for this. `--liveness-database-url` gives these two, and nothing else the worker sends, a URL of their own; without it they use `JELLYSCALE_DATABASE_URL`, and every other statement of the worker (claims, looks, registration, job rows, hints, scans, sweeps, migrations) goes there. The liveness URL must be a direct, session-mode connection: behind a transaction-mode pooler such as pgbouncer the lock would belong to whichever server connection the pooler chose, which keeps it after the worker is gone, so a killed worker is not noticed and its jobs wait their lease (observed with pgbouncer 1.26.1), and a worker that finds its lock missing says so in its log, naming `--liveness-database-url`, and tries again every second. The main URL may sit behind a pooler in session mode (observed with every role running behind pgbouncer in session mode and the liveness URL direct); in transaction or statement mode the hint listener's LISTEN and the advisory locks of the migrations, the scans and the sweeps, which are taken in one statement and released in another, belong to whichever server connection the pooler chose, so hints are lost and a lock can be held for good or by two replicas at once. A database slower than half a second a round trip is slow, not gone: the worker registers (it gives that 3 seconds) and keeps working, but each of its looks times out, so as the reaper it reaps nobody (another takes the role when it stops renewing) and finds its own lost lock only through its 10 second lease; and if it stays that slow for 10 seconds, the worker's `seen_at` refresh (also bounded at half a second) does not land and PlaybackInfo stops counting the worker until it does (`TestAWorkerRegistersOverASlowDatabase` uses 300 ms). A scan, a segment sweep and an asset sweep pass each hold their session advisory locks on one more connection of their own (one for all that a sweep pass deletes), opened outside the pool from the main URL's settings and not the liveness URL's, for as long as they run, so that the pool stays whole for their work (a small pool would otherwise wait for itself). Behind a session-mode pooler these pass through it, like the pool's. Postgres's `idle_session_timeout` must be off for the workers' role, since the lock's connection is idle by design: with it on, every timeout ends the lock and restarts the worker's transcodes. A forgotten worker's subtitle extractions and chapter pictures are cut with its jobs: their lease is left half a second (the `forgottenLease`) by the statement that deletes its row, so another worker takes them over about a second after a kill and the request that waited gets its track; where no connection closes (a host that loses power, a partition) their lease, 10 seconds, is the fallback, as it is for a transcode job.

An `api` replica killed mid-film loses neither the picture nor the resume position (JS-031, `docs/status.md`). Not built: an autoscaler.

## The viewer's audio language

A viewer's saved `AudioLanguagePreference` and `PlayDefaultAudioTrack` choose the track a film starts with, as Jellyfin 12.4 does (`compat/captures/audio-default-12.4.md`), comparing languages by language (`ger`, `deu` and `de-DE` are one). PlaybackInfo names it in `DefaultAudioStreamIndex`, and so does the item, whose menu jellyfin-web's Play button sends back as `AudioStreamIndex`, except where no transcode can run for as long as the state lasts (no `transcodeStore`, no probed duration, no live worker that runs transcodes, the roles' stores differ): there the item names the track a browser will hear, the file's default, while PlaybackInfo for a client that switches tracks itself still names and plays the saved-language track (D-034). A browser plays only the file's default track (the flagged one, else the first). So a preferred track that is not the file's default turns a browser's direct play into a copied stream: the video is copied where it can be (above) and the preferred track is carried, which takes a worker slot. Jellyfin does the same for a browser, whose profile has an `IsSecondaryAudio` condition (`SecondaryAudioNotSupported`). A client whose profile has no such condition, such as Octave, whose mpv switches the track itself, direct plays and pays no slot, and `DefaultAudioStreamIndex` names the preferred track. Where the copy cannot start (no worker is free, or no replica has a `transcodeStore`), the saved language falls back to the file's default track, which plays, and `DefaultAudioStreamIndex` names it. In the states that last (no `transcodeStore`, no probed duration, no live worker that runs transcodes, differing stores) the item and PlaybackInfo list that track alone, so a browser's audio menu offers one track and a pick of another is refused (D-029); while a worker has no cores free for a moment, or that spec's transcode just failed, every track is listed and a pick ends in the dialog above.

## Video copy

When the video can play in the client as it is and only the audio or the container can't (H.264, HEVC or AV1 with AC3 in MKV, or in MP4), the worker copies the video, and copies the audio too when the client takes its codec in fMP4 HLS within its channel limit and a quarter of the cap (as for audio copy below), else re-encodes it to AAC. A stream then costs almost no CPU and keeps the source's picture exactly. Nothing to configure. It happens when:

- the video is H.264, HEVC or AV1, and passes the client's `CodecProfiles` for it (profile, level, range, interlacing, resolution), and the client's HLS transcoding profile lists the codec;
- the picture is SDR (H.264 too), or it is Dolby Vision with a base layer the client plays (profiles 7, 8.1, 8.2, 8.4, 10, as Jellyfin 12.4 does: the master playlist names the base layer in `CODECS` and `VIDEO-RANGE`, and the Dolby Vision brand in `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`), and for HEVC and AV1 the profile is one the master playlist can name: HEVC Main or Main 10, AV1 profile 0 at 8 or 10 bits. HDR10, HDR10+ and HLG HEVC and AV1 are copied to a client whose profile shows the range (the master says `VIDEO-RANGE=PQ` or `HLG`). Otherwise they, and other profiles, are re-encoded to H.264, and HDR is tone mapped (below). Dolby Vision profile 5 is re-encoded to H.264, never copied or direct played for a client that does not declare Dolby Vision, whether its colours come out right is not observed, and JS-149 settles it (D-021);
- no bitrate cap is below the file's bitrate, and no subtitle is to be burnt in;
- the file has a keyframe index, which the scan reads from the file's own index (Matroska Cues, or the MP4 sample tables) and keeps in the database. A Matroska file written without Cues, a fragmented MP4 and any other container are re-encoded, as before;
- no gap between keyframes is longer than 12 seconds, counting the stretch from the last keyframe to the end of the film. x264 and x265 default to a keyframe every 250 frames, which is under 12 seconds at 21 fps and up. A file with longer gaps is re-encoded.

The playlist is cut on the file's keyframes: a segment ends at the first keyframe at least 4 seconds after it began, or earlier if waiting would make it longer than 12 seconds, so no segment is over 12 seconds, and the playlist says their exact lengths.

**A copy whose keyframe index is found wrong** (JS-088). A segment that does not start or end on a keyframe of the file means the index the playlist was cut from is wrong. jellyfin-web drops the stream on a failed segment once playback has begun, so the viewer is not left with the failure: the worker empties the index (no new PlaybackInfo offers the copy; the old index stays as `media_sources.dropped_keyframes`), and a request for a segment of the copy URL is answered from the playlist the viewer holds. A segment the transcode store holds is the copy's own; any other is made by an encode of the copy's codec (x264 for H.264, x265 for HEVC, SVT-AV1 for AV1) whose keyframes are forced at the playlist's own segment starts, so segment N starts and ends where the playlist says, and the viewer's audio track is the one it maps. The fallback is capped at 1080 lines like every other re-encode, with the source's aspect ratio kept. Each segment is sent with the init segment of its stream in front, so the player decodes it under whichever init it holds. The `stream` replica's log says `a copied stream falls back to a re-encode` (item, source, reason), and its `jellyscale_playback_copy_fallbacks_total` counts once for each stream. **An AV1 copy goes on as AV1** (JS-133). jellyfin-web's hls.js, observed (`compat/captures/av1-fallback-12.4.md`, `av1-fallback-spike.mjs`), keeps the SourceBuffer typed by the master playlist's `CODECS av01`, and Chrome refuses an H.264 segment appended to it (`CHUNK_DEMUXER_ERROR_APPEND_FAILED`, a fatal error and a dialog), so the fallback of an AV1 copy is an SVT-AV1 encode (`Spec.Recode` `av1`), for every AV1 copy, 8 or 10 bit, each segment led by the init segment of its own stream like the other fallbacks. SVT-AV1 in ffmpeg 6.1 ignores `-force_key_frames`, so the segments are not cut by forcing keyframes in one run: each segment of the playlist is made by an ffmpeg of its own (`AV1SegmentCommand`) that starts at the segment's first frame, stops at the next segment's, and so starts on a keyframe on every ffmpeg; the job goes on into the next segment with a new one (a restart at any segment makes the segments a run from the start does). The audio is cut as a restart of an encode is (`aacCut`): AAC packets on the grid of a run from zero, copied packets in the span of the video's. The run for a segment is held back at the viewers' lead as a running ffmpeg is (`room`). It has no helpers. The picture is capped at 1080 lines like any re-encode.

**An HDR copy's fallback keeps the picture's range** (JS-134). The fallback of an HDR10, HLG, HDR10+ or Dolby Vision copy (a base layer of PQ or HLG) is not tone mapped: its HEVC or AV1 encode is the copy's own picture at 10 bits, `format=yuv420p10le` and no tone-mapping filter, with no tag named, because the decoded frames carry the copy's primaries, transfer, matrix and range and both encoders write them (observed in `compat/captures/hdr-fallback-12.4.md`, with the commands that reproduce it). jellyfin-web's player was seen to take it in Chromium for a 10-bit PQ AV1 copy, in both directions across the change and under a 10-bit codec string, with the copy and the fallback at one video timescale (the same file); HEVC (this Chromium plays no `hvc1`), HLG and Dolby Vision were not observed in a browser. The e2e tier plays the AV1 case on the real stack, where the copy and the fallback have different timescales. An H.264 fallback, and the fallback of an SDR copy of any codec and bit depth, are as they were: 8 bits, and tone mapped for an HDR source. A Dolby Vision copy falls back by its base layer's transfer (profiles 7, 8.1 and 10.1 are PQ, 8.4 is HLG, so they keep 10 bits; 8.2 has an SDR base layer and goes on as an SDR copy). The spec carries it as `Spec.HDR` (the transfer), apart from `ToneMap`, which stays "an encode that maps to SDR"; it is part of the hash. Dolby Vision profile 5 has no base layer of PQ or HLG and is never copied, so it has no such fallback: it is re-encoded to H.264, whether its colours come out right is not observed, and JS-149 settles it (D-021).

HDR fallback keeps: 10 bits (Main 10 for HEVC, profile 0 at 10 bits for AV1), colour primaries, transfer, matrix and range, as the copy has them.
HDR fallback gap: the HDR10 mastering display is lost on ffmpeg 6.1 (the encode does not write it) and kept from ffmpeg 9, so the init segment differs with the worker's ffmpeg.
HDR fallback gap: the HDR10 content light level is lost on ffmpeg 6.1 and kept from ffmpeg 9, as the mastering display.
HDR fallback gap: HDR10+ dynamic metadata is lost; the picture goes on as its HDR10 base.
HDR fallback gap: Dolby Vision dynamic metadata (the RPU, and the enhancement layer of profile 7) is lost; the picture goes on as its base layer's HDR10 or HLG.
HDR fallback gap: lines above 1080 are lost: a 4K copy's fallback is 1920x1080 like every re-encode.

A job that fails for a cause that is not the file's is made again as the same kind of job: one request starts at most three fresh jobs, and `ensure` and PlaybackInfo count the spec's such failures (`transcode_jobs.again`) that ended within `failureMemory` (30 seconds), so a fourth failure makes them the spec's remembered failure, whichever requests the jobs were started for. hls.js abandons a request at 10 seconds and asks again, and the next one starts no job: it gets the recorded cause and PlaybackInfo refuses it, with no write from the stream role. The cause a failed job names is what the store gave back to ffmpeg: a body cut that `store.Reader` resumed is never seen by the job and cannot replace ffmpeg's own error or a wrong index (`store.InputWithFailure`).

The `master.m3u8` and `main.m3u8` carry the token in their lines only when the request carried it in the query (as `ApiKey` or `api_key`, with the request's own token; any other value is not echoed). A client that sends its token in a header must send that header on every playlist and segment request. `TranscodingUrl`, the `DeliveryUrls` and the subtitle playlist still carry the token.

A copy is not capped, and at Auto it carries the file's whole bitrate and size, 4K included, exactly as direct play of the same file does and as Jellyfin's copy does. A viewer on a slow link picks a cap in the quality menu, which re-encodes. A copy stores and sends the picture at the file's own bitrate: a 40 Mbps remux takes about 18 GB per hour (computed) of film for each spec, kept for 24 hours, against the 5.7 GB per hour at most of a 1080p re-encode. None of the storage ceilings above applies to it.

Reading the source: a copy of both the video and the audio opens the file once and reads it at 5 times the film's speed (`-readrate 5`), so while the job runs it reads five times the film's bitrate, and a run to the end reads the film's bytes once. A copy of the video whose audio is re-encoded opens the file twice, once for each (the AAC encoder starts on its own grid), so it reads ten times the film's bitrate and a run to the end twice the film's bytes; the figures below are for that path, and halve for a copy of both. A job also stops when it is far enough ahead of its viewers (below), so what it reads is bounded by what is watched, not by the film. Every request for a copied stream's playlists and segments reads the film's keyframe array (about 14 KB computed for a 2 hour film with a keyframe every 4 seconds, 8 bytes each) from Postgres; the request rate has not been measured. For a 2 hour film at 40 Mbps (36 GB), a viewer who watches it to the end still costs a run to the end: 72 GB read from the source (72 GB of S3 egress), 36 GB of segments stored for 24 hours, over the length of the film. A viewer who leaves after a minute no longer costs the 120 seconds of a job running free (about 600 s of film made, 6 GB read, 3 GB stored, computed from the rates). The job has made at most the furthest segment the player asked for, plus the 60 seconds of `transcodeLead`, plus the rest of its last segment (up to 12 seconds): with a player that asked for 30 seconds beyond what it showed (an assumption; the buffer was not measured), 60 + 30 + 60 + 12 = 162 seconds of film, 1.6 GB read, 0.8 GB stored, and then the job waits `transcodeIdle` paused, reading nothing. The byte figures here are computed from the bitrate and the length, not measured. They assume the store takes uploads at 5 times the film's speed (200 Mbps for a 40 Mbps film), and ffmpeg is not paced by uploads: unuploaded segments sit in `transcodeScratch` a few at a time if the store keeps up. A store slower than that lets ffmpeg make segments in scratch until the uploaded ones reach the lead, which is more than the lead by the ratio of the speeds (5 times the lead for a store that takes the film at 1 times its speed); they are not uploaded past the lead. A seek waits for the whole segment it lands in, up to 12 seconds of film (60 MB at 40 Mbps), read at 5 times the film's speed and downloaded at the file's bitrate.

**Read-ahead.** A job makes at most `transcodeLead` (default 60 seconds of film, 15 segments of 4 seconds in a re-encode, fewer in a copy whose segments are longer) beyond the furthest segment a viewer of its spec has asked for. Then the worker stops its ffmpeg (SIGSTOP) and starts it again (SIGCONT) when requests come within the lead. The stream replica that serves a segment writes the segment number into the viewer's row, `transcode_viewers.segment`, in the write it already makes for every request (`last_seen_at`), so a segment request costs no more writes than before. A worker whose job has made everything up to its limit looks at the viewers' segments every 500 ms; a job that is not at its limit does not look. The viewers counted are those not stopped and seen within `transcodeIdle`, whose last segment is one this job has made or is about to (the window in which `ensure` lets a request join a job): a viewer far ahead has a job of its own and does not carry this one on. A viewer that has fetched only a playlist, or one served by a replica from before this, has no segment and frees the job until it asks for one or is gone. A paused job keeps its lease, its cores and its scratch directory, which the worker touches so that another worker's sweep leaves it; the stall check does not run while it is paused; and `transcodeIdle` ends it as it ends any job, 60 seconds after the last request. A seek past the lead starts a job there, as before. Segments beyond the limit that ffmpeg made before it was paused stay in scratch until the limit reaches them. SIGSTOP was chosen over ffmpeg's own controls because it is the same on ffmpeg 6.1, 8.1 and 9, needs no pipe to ffmpeg, and leaves its input open at its place. A system without it (not Linux) runs jobs without the bound and says so in the log.

Default lead: the bound is the furthest segment asked for plus the lead, not the playback position, so what the player asks for is always inside it, whatever its buffer. The lead only has to cover what a resume takes: the worker's 500 ms look, ffmpeg making the next segment (a re-encode slower than real time makes a 4 second one in more than 4 seconds), and the delay between the player asking and the segment landing. In jellyfin-web in Chromium with `transcodeLead` 20s (5 segments), a copied film's job had stored 7 segments when the player had asked for up to 2, and a re-encoded one's 10 when it had asked for up to 5, of 45, with ffmpeg stopped (`test/e2e`). With 10 second copied segments from S3 at 5 times the film's speed, a request after a 20 second pause was answered in 2.2 to 2.4 seconds, and each one after it in 1.9 (`TestAPausedCopyFromS3ResumesAndMakesTheSameSegments`).

Jellyfin 12.4 (a `jellyfin/jellyfin:12.4` container's `encoding.xml`) has `EnableThrottling` false and `ThrottleDelaySeconds` 180 by default, so its transcodes run to the end of the film unless the operator turns throttling on. How it pauses when on was not observed; jellyscale bounds by default.

**Sizing a worker.** A worker declares its cores (`transcodeCores`), and each transcode job costs some of them, fixed by what it does to which source and the same on every replica and worker. A paused job holds its cores as one that is reading does, so PlaybackInfo's answer does not change with whether a watched job happens to be paused, and a copy of a 2 hour film holds its place for the 2 hours. A job is one spec being watched: viewers of the same spec share it, direct play takes none. From the measurements under Tone mapping (x264 veryfast, at most 1080 lines out), a re-encode costs what it does to its source, not to its output, so a 4K source costs its 4K figure although the output is at most 1080 lines, and a source taller than 1080 lines never costs less than the 1080p figure (a source over 1088 lines is priced as 4K):

| Job | Cores |
|---|---|
| Re-encode of a 1080p SDR source | 0.8 |
| Re-encode of a 4K SDR source | 1.6 |
| Re-encode of a 1080p source tone mapped (HDR) | 2.0 |
| Re-encode of a 4K source tone mapped (HDR) | 3.2 |
| HEVC fallback re-encode of a 1080p SDR source | 3.2 |
| HEVC fallback re-encode of a 4K SDR source | 4.0 |
| AV1 fallback re-encode of a 1080p SDR source | 2.0 |
| AV1 fallback re-encode of a 4K SDR source | 2.4 |
| HEVC fallback re-encode of a 1080p HDR source | 3.2 |
| HEVC fallback re-encode of a 4K HDR source | 4.0 |
| AV1 fallback re-encode of a 1080p HDR source | 3.6 |
| AV1 fallback re-encode of a 4K HDR source | 4.2 |
| A copy, its audio copied or re-encoded | 0.05 |

The HEVC fallback costs its corresponding x264 row plus 2.4 (less for an output under 1080p, in proportion to its pixels), from the measurement under Video copy above; an HDR copy's is not tone mapped, so its row is the SDR one's. An HDR copy's AV1 fallback is priced as the tone-mapped one was (3.6 and 4.2), above the CPU measured for it (the HDR rows under Video copy). Its output is capped at 1080 lines like any other re-encode.

A job fits a worker when the costs of the jobs it holds and of this one add up to no more than its cores (a 4 core worker holds five 1080p SDR re-encodes, and a 4K tone mapped one beside one 1080p SDR), and PlaybackInfo offers a transcode whenever some one worker of the output version has the job's cost free: two workers with 2 cores free each do not hold a 3.2 core job. A job queued and not yet claimed counts, on the worker with most cores free, and a worker never claims a job that would take it over its cores; a worker without room for the oldest queued job leaves it for one that has, and takes a later job that fits. A job that costs more than the cores of every worker is refused at once. Copies are bounded apart from cores by a count per worker, 32, because a copy costs almost nothing but still reads and writes a stream; the log names the bound when every worker is at it. Helpers (Parallel transcoding) never take a worker over its cores, and a new viewer stops as many of one worker's helpers as its job needs, never a viewer's job. A worker that declares nothing, as one of the release before, takes the processors it may use (never fewer than 3.2 cores); size `transcodeCores` as the cores of the machine, less a quarter for headroom, because a new job and each seek run flat out until they are the lead ahead. Too few cores show as `cores are short` in the log, with the job's cost, and a "cannot be played at this time" dialog. The queue's cost in cores, `jellyscale_transcode_queue_cores`, is what an autoscaler should follow (docs/running.md). A paused job whose viewers have all seeked away from it is cancelled within a second and gives its cores back; jellyfin-web stops the old stream when it switches audio track or quality (`Sessions/Playing/Stopped`), which ends its job; one whose viewers have gone (a closed tab sends nothing) is ended `transcodeIdle` after their last request, as any job is. Workers and replicas roll in any order: a worker of this release keeps a positive `slots` in its row beside its cores, so a replica of the release before still counts it, and a worker of the release before is counted at its slots as cores, and a job queued by such a replica at its spec's cost.

**Paused jobs and ffmpeg 6.1.** ffmpeg 6.1 budgets `-readrate` against the wall clock without `-readrate_catchup`, so after a pause it reads unthrottled until it has caught up (measured: `-readrate 5 -c copy` stopped for 8 seconds at 20.4 seconds of film was at 61 seconds 0.2 seconds after SIGCONT; 8.1.3 and 9.0.2 went on at 5 times the film). The worker pauses it again within its 100 ms poll, so on 6.1 a resume reads past the limit what ffmpeg reads at full speed in up to 100 ms, bounded by the disk or the network rather than the film. Nothing past the limit is stored. The image's ffmpeg is not 6.1; a worker run on Ubuntu 24.04's is. **S3 inputs.** A stopped ffmpeg stops reading its two connections to the loopback server, which stop reading their S3 GETs. The test pauses a copy from SeaweedFS and Garage for 20 seconds and resumes it with the same segments, byte for byte, as a run from the start; a body the store cuts meanwhile is reopened at its offset by the reader (`store.Reader`, invariant 42). A connection a proxy drops with a reset meanwhile is reopened at its offset the same way (`TestReaderResumesAfterAReset`, with a store that resets the body; no real proxy was run), so a job paused for up to `transcodeIdle` with its GETs held does not fail on that. **Upgrading.** The migration adds `transcode_viewers.segment`. Replicas and workers may roll in any order: a worker keeps a job unbounded while a viewer of its spec has recorded no segment, as an old `api` or `stream` replica never does.

The audio becomes stereo AAC (192 kbps by default), so only the picture is unchanged.

The scan builds the index, and a few things have to be known about it. A film scanned before this version has no index until the next scan reads it (a scan does so for every H.264, HEVC or AV1 file in MKV or MP4 that has none), so it is re-encoded until then. For a Matroska file the index is the Cues (the test bounds the read to 2% of a small test file); for an MP4 it is the moov, which has an entry for every frame and audio packet, so a large MP4's is megabytes. The scan also reads where the video ends, from the packets of the last stretch from the last keyframe to the end of the file, when that stretch is within 12 seconds of the file's duration (a film with a longer gap can't be copied and is not read): up to 12 seconds of film, read up to twice (120 MB at 40 Mbps), plus what ffmpeg reads to open the file, a few MB that vary with its version. If the index turns out not to match the file (a segment doesn't start or end on a keyframe), the job fails, the file's index is dropped and an error logged with the item and source, and the next PlaybackInfo re-encodes the film; a viewer already playing goes on through the fallback above (H.264, HEVC and AV1, JS-133). A dropped index stays empty until the file changes; to have the scan read it again, set `keyframes` to NULL on that `media_sources` row. An input cut short (a file truncated in the store) fails the job but keeps the index, unless the cut is within 1.5 seconds of the end of the film, inside the last segment: that job succeeds and its last segment is that much short. The scan logs an unusable index at Info with the reason, and a read that failed at Warn, to be tried again at the next scan; the other reasons a film is re-encoded are in the `TranscodeReasons` of the PlaybackInfo reply. A film whose audio outlasts its video enough to fail the 12 second check against the file's duration is re-encoded.

Upgrading from a version without video copy, roll in this order: workers, then `stream` replicas, then `api` replicas. The API is what offers a copy, so it goes last. A worker from before this release that claimed a copy job would not know it is a copy, and would re-encode the picture and store it under the copy's hash; once every worker is new, none can.

After the HEVC and AV1 slice, HEVC and AV1 films scanned before it have no index and no stored bit depth until the next scan reads them (it does so without the file changing), and are re-encoded until then. Chrome plays AV1 in software, so an AV1 film with AC3 audio now arrives as a copy of the file's own picture; it plays HEVC only with a hardware decoder, which jellyfin-web's profile reports, so on other machines an HEVC film is re-encoded as before. What a copy costs in Postgres, network and storage is the H.264 copy's or less (HEVC and AV1 files are smaller than H.264 of the same quality): the keyframe array per request, the Cues or moov read once per scan, no new role. Observed against Jellyfin 12.4 in [`compat/captures/hevc-av1-12.4.md`](../compat/captures/hevc-av1-12.4.md).

After this release, existing H.264 films whose audio the client can't play (AC3 in MKV, say) become full-bitrate copies once a scan has read their index, where they were re-encoded. A viewer on such a film gets the source's picture, and the storage and bandwidth change with it: a copy stores and sends the file's own bitrate (see Storage above and the figures under Video copy), where a re-encode is at most 1080p.

**Audio copy.** When the video is re-encoded (a codec the client lacks, interlaced, a level or profile over its limit, a bitrate cap, a burnt subtitle) the audio is copied, not encoded to AAC, if the client's fMP4 HLS transcoding profile lists its codec (of AAC, MP3, AC3, E-AC3, Opus and FLAC), the track has no more channels than that profile's `MaxAudioChannels` and the CodecProfile limits on audio, and it takes no more than a quarter of the bitrate cap (the copy's own bitrate comes out of the cap and the picture gets the rest). Jellyfin's Chrome profile lists AAC, Opus and FLAC with two channels, so there stereo tracks of those codecs copy and AC3 and 5.1 are still encoded to stereo AAC; a client that lists AC3 and six channels gets them as they are, when the track is within a quarter of the cap. A track whose file doesn't state its bitrate (a Matroska file without a `BPS` tag, which the scan now reads) counts as 6 Mbps, so it copies at Auto and under caps of 24 Mbps and up; a film scanned before this version has the bitrates the earlier scan stored, with no rescan forced. The video-copy path still encodes its audio. Observed against Jellyfin 12.4 in [`compat/captures/audio-copy-12.4.md`](../compat/captures/audio-copy-12.4.md). After a restart on another worker, a copy from an MP4 has the same audio bytes in each segment; a copy from a Matroska file has the same packets, with times that can move by under 1 ms, because Matroska stores whole milliseconds. That is inaudible, and accepted.

What it costs: a worker spends no CPU on the audio (no decode, downmix or AAC encode) and a restart reads the film the same two times as before. The network, S3 reads and stored segments grow with the track: a 640 kbps AC3 5.1 track in place of 192 kbps AAC is 0.45 Mbps more to each viewer, so 100 streams add about 45 Mbps through `stream` and from the store, and about 0.2 GB of storage per hour of film for each spec (viewers of the same film, track and cap share its segments) (FLAC at 1 to 4 Mbps adds up to ten times that), kept for the 24 hours of any segment. Postgres holds no more than before: a stream's `BitRate` was already in `media_sources.streams`, and the spec hash text is a few characters longer. A copied track is the file's own packets, so a restart on another worker puts the same packets in the same segments (`TestCopiedAudioSplicesTheSamePackets`), where encoded audio differs inaudibly; an MP4 source's audio in each segment is byte for byte the same, and a Matroska source's packets are identical with times that can differ by under 1 ms after a restart, because Matroska stores whole milliseconds (accepted). The risk that is not measured: a client that lists FLAC or AC3 whose decoder stutters on a high bitrate; its CodecProfile limits are honoured, nothing else is.

The scan reads the head and the Cues of a Matroska film with an image track, a few hundred KB, once. Each cue point also keeps the time of the last video keyframe the Cues place at or before its cluster, because a muxer can write a packet well before the video of its time (ffmpeg 6.1 does for a track it reads from another file, so a plain seek to the cut lands after the packet and never reads it); a run opens at the earlier of that and the cue's first packet, which costs nothing more in a file that is interleaved by time. A cue that is never cleared is showing for the rest of the film, and a run long after it pays for all of that age: there is no cap. No check passes or fails on a duration.

### The stored track (JS-089)

A play that burns an image track inside the film has one worker job read the film once and store each image track as a Matroska file of that one track in the asset store (DOMAINS 51: key `subtitles/{source}/{hash of the ETag}/{index}.mkv`, kind `images`, `ffmpeg -copyts -map 0:N -c:s copy -f matroska`). A run that begins mid-film draws the cue showing at its cut from that file, which is a **second input** of its ffmpeg, and opens the film at its cut as a film with no subtitle: the cue's age costs kilobytes of the stored track and no film read.

**Spike: how the two inputs align.** Tried by hand on ffmpeg 6.1.1 (Ubuntu 24.04's, through `test/ffmpeg61.sh`) and ffmpeg 9.0.2, then by the checks (`image_store_pictures_integration_test.go`) on both. The film is opened with `-copyts -ss {cut}`, and the stored track as `-canvas_size WxH -i pipe:0` with no `-ss` and no `-itsoffset`: its packets are at the film's own times (`-copyts` in the extraction), which `-copyts` keeps in the run, so the two inputs are aligned by construction, where a subtitle file beside the film (cue times from 0) needs `-itsoffset` by the film's start time. A cue that began before the cut has its packet before the first video frame, and the overlay draws it from that frame on both versions. The stream begins at the cluster of the first packet of the picture showing at the cut (`library.ImageTrack.Feed`, which finds it by the classification the film's own cue points use), so no seek of the track is needed. What each version needed: ffmpeg refuses a Matroska stream whose Segment is shorter than it says, so the Segment is given the size "unknown" in the head that is fed; and it refuses one with no cluster at all, so a cut after the last packet begins the stream at the last packet, which ended before the cut. The frame a cue appears and ends on is the same as in a run from zero, frame by frame at both edges, for a Matroska film (PGS, VobSub) and an MPEG-TS one (DVB, whose clock does not start at zero) on both versions: the stored times are Matroska's milliseconds, so a cue edge could differ by a frame only if it lay within half a millisecond of a frame boundary, which was not tried. The track is given to ffmpeg through a pipe of one page that a goroutine fills from the asset store as ffmpeg takes it, in pieces of 8 KB: a loopback server over `store.Reader`, as the film has, reads ahead as far as the kernel's buffers allow, which the task's design review measured at about twice a track of 210 KB for each start of ffmpeg (not measured again here, and not on ffmpeg 6.1).

**Rolling.** An old worker (from before JS-089) does not know the job kind `images`: it fails the job as a subtitle track that is not this file's (`subtitle extraction failed`, logged at Error) and the next play after 30 seconds queues it again. Roll the workers first (docs/running.md): with new workers running, only they make the track, and a play meanwhile draws as JS-071 does. The extraction has slots of its own (2 at a time, none of the 4 a viewer's subtitle uses), and a read that outlasts the one hour time limit is failed for good until the daily sweep or the file's change, not asked for again on each seek (docs/running.md).

**What a seek costs** (through a worker's job, `go test -tags integration`, a scratch benchmark over the checks' kit, not kept; the film and the way of measuring are those of the table above: 34 MB, 128 s of 640x360 noise at 2 Mbps, a PGS track with a cue at 4 to 8 s and one from 56 s to 120 s, served by the store at 8 MB/s; the bytes the film's store had served when the first segment was stored, and the time to first segment from the request; each figure is the median of three runs; origin/main is the tree this task started from, measured the same way):

| Seek to | Cue showing | Without the track | origin/main, burnt | This build, before the track is stored | This build, after the track is stored |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 s | no cue showing (the last ended 32 s before) | 6.2 MB, 0.81 s | 6.2 MB, 0.80 s | 6.2 MB, 0.80 s | 6.2 MB, 0.82 s |
| 64 s | a cue 8 s old | 6.2 MB, 0.80 s | 8.9 MB, 1.11 s | 8.9 MB, 1.22 s | 6.3 MB, 0.82 s |

## Tone mapping

A film in PQ (HDR10) or HLG that is re-encoded to H.264 is tone mapped to SDR BT.709 on the worker, so a viewer on an SDR screen doesn't see washed-out colour. Nothing to configure. It happens when:

- the video is re-encoded, which an HDR film is for a client that wasn't told it plays HDR (an HDR film is never copied to one, in H.264 as in HEVC and AV1);
- the file declares PQ or HLG as its transfer function. Dolby Vision is mapped by its base layer: 8.1 as HDR10 and 8.4 as HLG. Films scanned before this release have no transfer function stored and are not mapped until the next scan reads them again. Dolby Vision profile 5, which has no HDR10 base, is re-encoded to H.264, whether its colours come out right is not observed, and JS-149 settles it (D-021).

It is done in software with ffmpeg's `zscale` and `tonemap` filters, which every ffmpeg the project is tested with has (6.1.1 on Ubuntu 24.04, 8.1 on Alpine, 9.0). `libplacebo` would need a GPU on the worker and is not used. Tone mapping is the expensive part of an HDR transcode: on this 16 core machine, x264 veryfast to 1080p cost 40.5 CPU seconds for 20 s of 1080p HDR10 against 15.2 without the mapping (2.7 times), and 63.7 CPU seconds for 20 s of 4K HEVC against 32.2 (2.0 times), about 3.2 cores to keep one 4K HDR stream in real time. A 4K HDR re-encode is costed at 3.2 cores for this reason (see Sizing a worker). The output is bigger than an unmapped re-encode of the same film, by 11% to 16% for the 1080p clip and 16% to 38% for the 4K one in two measurements (11.9 MB against 10.7 MB, and 6.4 MB against 5.5 MB), under the same bitrate cap, 8-bit, at most 1080 lines.

Upgrading from a version without tone mapping, roll in this order: workers, then `stream` replicas, then `api` replicas. Both `stream` and `api` replicas queue tone-mapped jobs (a master playlist request starts one). A worker from before this release that claimed one would not know the spec's `ToneMap`, would re-encode the picture without mapping it and store the washed-out segments under the tone-mapped hash, where viewers get them for the 24 hours of retention with nothing in any log. The same holds when rolling back: roll back the `api` and `stream` replicas first, so no tone-mapped job is queued when the first old worker starts. Once every worker is new, none can claim one wrongly. This is a known departure (ARCHITECTURE, Known departures), the same exposure as copied audio, so workers roll first and roll back last. A `stream` replica of this release still serves an older `api` replica's copy of an H.264 HDR film as a copy, so a playback begun during the upgrade keeps going.

Measured 2026-11-21 on a 4K Dolby Vision (profile 7) HEVC Main 10 PQ film with a PGS track, 120 s and 60 s windows, through the worker's own command (2160 HDR to 1920x1080 SDR H.264, tone mapped, the PGS track burned in), three runs each.

## A helper whose viewers have seeked away (JS-167)

JS-162 found that a helper whose viewer seeked away went on alone: `furthest` found no viewer at or before its chunk, returned -1, and `limitFor(layout, -1, lead)` is the film's end, so the helper's limit was lifted to the end of the film and it held a worker for it (5 min 25 s in one run). A helper looked at the viewers only while at its limit, so a helper running short of it never noticed.

Built (D-046): a helper reads the viewers every 500 ms (`leadCheck`) for as long as it runs. It is stopped, its row cancelled before its ffmpeg is ended, when no viewer is left for it: every viewer has made a forward seek beyond it (none at or before its chunk, or within `catchUpSegments` of its next segment past its chunk, where a request joins it, invariant 37), or has made a backward seek so far (the middle of the film to its first minute) that the lead past the furthest of them ends before the helper's first segment. A viewer playing on past its chunk into segments the helper makes bounds it, as one at or before its chunk does, and no helper stores a lead past the furthest segment asked for. A viewer who steps back but stays within a lead of the helper's first segment keeps it; a failed read of the viewers is neither a reason to stop it nor to lift its limit; a viewer that asked for no segment frees it while it is seen. What it stored stays stored, and a viewer coming back to the chunk gets a job at the first missing segment. It is counted under `helper_left_behind` in `jellyscale_transcode_jobs_ended_total` and logged once.

Measured 2026-11-21 on the developer machine (AMD Ryzen 7 7800X3D, ffmpeg 9.0.2) with JS-162's harness, adapted: before is 45fa298, after f0bde61, each built from `git archive`.
